Why Rights Must Come First

A Response to Sunaina Nassa's UCC Debate
Author: Vinayak Koli Course: SSH · IIIT Delhi Roll: 2023597

Sunaina Nassa's article, "The Debate over Uniform Civil Code (UCC) and its Significance for India as a Secular Democracy," provides a thoughtful and balanced perspective on one of India's most sensitive and long-standing constitutional questions. Nassa's argument is neither a simple endorsement nor a rejection of the UCC. Instead, she approaches it as a matter of balancing two essential principles: the need to respect and preserve religious and cultural diversity and the constitutional obligation to uphold equality and individual rights.

I agree with Nassa's emphasis on the constitutional vision of equality and her acknowledgment of the existing discrimination within personal laws. Across religions, women have historically faced unequal treatment in matters of marriage, inheritance, divorce, and guardianship. Nassa is right to point out that such practices contradict the idea of gender justice and weaken India's democratic ideals.

However, I differ from Nassa in how the concept of "balance" should be understood. While she calls for a "harmonious balance" between cultural identity and equality, I argue that balance should never come at the cost of justice. Equality, dignity, and liberty are non-negotiable values that cannot be compromised to protect cultural traditions. India's challenge, therefore, is not choosing between uniformity and diversity but ensuring that no law, custom, or tradition violates the Constitution.

The Central Question: Limits of Cultural Autonomy

Nassa's idea of balance raises a deeper constitutional and moral question that defines this entire debate:

Should community-based personal laws hold greater authority than the Fundamental Rights guaranteed by the Constitution?

The Indian Constitution provides a clear answer. Article 13 declares that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is void. Article 25, while protecting religious freedom, explicitly states that this freedom is subject to public order, morality, health, and the other provisions of Part III — the Fundamental Rights. This ensures that faith and culture can exist, but not at the cost of equality or justice.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, during the Constituent Assembly Debates, supported the idea of a Uniform Civil Code but proposed a voluntary adoption model. He suggested that individuals could choose to be governed by a common civil law, allowing reform to grow gradually through social consensus. However, Ambedkar also made it clear that the Constitution stands above all religious or customary practices.

This creates a necessary distinction between voluntarism in implementation and supremacy in principle. Ambedkar's opt-in model was a method to make reform peaceful, not a reason to tolerate injustice. In other words, the process may be voluntary, but justice cannot be.

Faith vs. Constitutional Authority

The Colonial Legacy and Ambedkar's Dilemma

During British rule, the Lex Loci Report of 1840 introduced a uniform criminal and contract law but left personal laws untouched to avoid offending religious sentiments. This created a dual legal system: one secular and one religious, which continued after independence. When India's framers debated the UCC, Dr. Ambedkar strongly supported it, but the trauma of Partition made many leaders cautious. To preserve peace, Article 44 was included in the Directive Principles of State Policy — expressing the "desirability" of a UCC but leaving it non-enforceable.

Inequality Across Personal Laws

India's personal law systems have historically reflected deep gender and social inequality across all religions. The issue is not with any single community, but with the principle of allowing personal laws to function outside constitutional review. The demand for reform is not "anti-minority"; it is pro-equality and pro-justice.

  • Hindu Personal Law: Before reforms (1951–56), practices like polygamy and denial of women's property rights were common. The Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act still names the father as the "natural guardian," making the mother secondary.
  • Muslim Personal Law: Muslim law remains largely uncodified, allowing outdated practices such as polygamy, unequal inheritance, and previously Triple Talaq (now banned). Women's rights in divorce, custody, and guardianship are still restricted.
  • Christian Personal Law: Until 2001, divorce laws were discriminatory, giving men more grounds for divorce than women. Reforms have since corrected this, but the delay shows how long gender inequality was justified under the guise of religion.
  • Parsi Personal Law: Women who marry outside the faith lose their religious and property rights, conflicting with Articles 14 and 15 of the Constitution.

Customary and Tribal Laws

The issue extends beyond religious personal laws to tribal and customary practices. In several tribal communities such as the Khasi, Garo, and Naga, women are denied equal inheritance rights or excluded from leadership positions. In 2016, the Nagaland State Women's Commission petitioned for 33% reservation for women in urban local bodies. Tribal groups opposed it, citing "customary law," but the courts held that customary law cannot override constitutional equality.

The Judiciary's Role

The judiciary has made it clear that no personal or customary law can claim supremacy over the Constitution:

  • In Shah Bano (1985), the Supreme Court upheld a Muslim woman's right to maintenance under Article 21, emphasizing that personal laws cannot deny dignity.
  • In Sarla Mudgal (1995), the Court ruled that conversion to Islam for the purpose of bigamy was unconstitutional under Article 14.
  • In Shayara Bano (2017), the Court struck down instant triple talaq, stating that it violated women's equality and dignity.
  • In Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020), the Supreme Court upheld that daughters have equal coparcenary rights in ancestral property.

Each of these cases reaffirmed one truth: the Constitution, not custom, is the ultimate source of justice.

Chief Justice Chandrachud's View

Chief Justice Chandrachud recently observed that the Constitution expresses the "desirability" of a Uniform Civil Code but emphasized that its implementation must be inclusive and legislative, rather than judicial. He stressed that reform must be guided by dialogue, consensus, and inclusion. The Constitution desires equality, but through persuasion, not force.

Balance Misunderstood

When Nassa speaks of "balance," it risks being interpreted as compromise, where justice is softened to preserve peace. But true balance is not about giving equal weight to religion and rights; it is about ensuring that religion operates within constitutional limits. Religious freedom must coexist with equality and dignity, not be above them.

The correct understanding of balance is not "Faith vs. Rights," but "Faith within Rights."

Why Fundamental Rights Must Come First

India's Constitution already contains the tools for reform: Article 13 voids laws that violate fundamental rights, Articles 32 and 226 give citizens the right to challenge such laws in court, and judicial review ensures the Constitution always prevails over custom.

India does not need to wait for a Uniform Civil Code to implement equality. The Constitution itself is the ultimate civil code of rights and freedoms.

India's progress toward equality has already been achieved through targeted reforms: the Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act of 2005 gave daughters equal inheritance rights; the Christian Divorce (Amendment) Act of 2001 ensured gender equality in divorce; and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act of 2019 criminalized instant triple talaq. These changes prove that reform does not require uniformity; it only requires constitutional commitment.

True secularism, as envisioned by Ambedkar and Nehru, is not about erasing religion but about ensuring justice within diversity. Religion may guide personal life, but it cannot authorize injustice. Equality, dignity, and liberty form the uniform minimum for all Indians — the foundation on which personal freedoms can stand.

Conclusion

Whether India enacts a Uniform Civil Code or continues reforming personal laws one by one, the ultimate principle must remain the same: fundamental rights are supreme.

Dr. Ambedkar's idea of voluntary reform was never meant to make equality optional; it was meant to achieve it peacefully. The Constitution already provides the moral and legal tools, as outlined in Articles 13, 14, 15, and 21, ensuring that no law or custom can stand if it violates equality, liberty, or human dignity.

Therefore, the debate should not be framed as UCC versus religious pluralism but as a reaffirmation of constitutional supremacy. Uniformity is only a tool; justice is the goal. Every law, personal or state-made, must bend to the higher authority of Fundamental Rights. They are, in essence, the real Uniform Civil Code that already unites all citizens under one Constitution.
