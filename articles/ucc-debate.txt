WHY RIGHTS MUST COME FIRST: A RESPONSE TO SUNAINA NASSA'S UCC DEBATE
By Vinayak Koli

---

1. AGREEMENT: SUPPORTING THE VISION OF EQUALITY, JUSTICE, AND UNITY

Sunaina Nassa's article, "The Debate over Uniform Civil Code (UCC) and its Significance for India as a Secular Democracy," provides a thoughtful and balanced perspective on one of India's most sensitive and long-standing constitutional questions. Nassa's argument is neither a simple endorsement nor a rejection of the UCC. Instead, she approaches it as a matter of balancing two essential principles: the need to respect and preserve religious and cultural diversity and the constitutional obligation to uphold equality and individual rights.

I agree with Nassa's emphasis on the constitutional vision of equality and her acknowledgment of the existing discrimination within personal laws. Across religions, women have historically faced unequal treatment in matters of marriage, inheritance, divorce, and guardianship. Nassa is right to point out that such practices contradict the idea of gender justice and weaken India's democratic ideals.

I also agree with her emphasis on inclusivity and social harmony. A country as diverse as India cannot pursue reform by disregarding cultural identity. Nassa's recognition of India's pluralism is crucial, and her call for gradual, inclusive change reflects constitutional sensitivity.

However, I differ from Nassa in how the concept of "balance" should be understood. While she calls for a "harmonious balance" between cultural identity and equality, I argue that balance should never come at the cost of justice. Equality, dignity, and liberty are non-negotiable values that cannot be compromised to protect cultural traditions. The Constitution already provides a clear path for reform, whether through a Uniform Civil Code or targeted legal changes within each religion; however, all reforms must be guided by the principle that fundamental rights are supreme.

India's challenge, therefore, is not choosing between uniformity and diversity but ensuring that no law, custom, or tradition violates the Constitution.

---

2. CENTRAL QUESTION: THE LIMITS OF CULTURAL AUTONOMY

Nassa's idea of balance raises a deeper constitutional and moral question that defines this entire debate:

Should community-based personal laws hold greater authority than the Fundamental Rights guaranteed by the Constitution?

This question is central to India's democratic identity. It asks whether religious autonomy can justify practices that violate equality and dignity, or whether all laws, including those rooted in religion, must be governed by constitutional principles.

The Indian Constitution provides a clear answer. Article 13 declares that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is void. Article 25, while protecting religious freedom, explicitly states that this freedom is subject to public order, morality, health, and the other provisions of Part III — that is, the Fundamental Rights. This ensures that faith and culture can exist, but not at the cost of equality or justice.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, during the Constituent Assembly Debates, supported the idea of a Uniform Civil Code but proposed a voluntary adoption model. He suggested that individuals could choose to be governed by a common civil law, allowing reform to grow gradually through social consensus. Ambedkar's model reflected his democratic understanding that social change must be accepted, not imposed.

However, Ambedkar also made it clear that the Constitution stands above all religious or customary practices. Article 13 of the Constitution declares that any law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is void. Article 25 guarantees freedom of religion but explicitly states that this freedom is subject to other Fundamental Rights.

This means that religion cannot be used as a shield to justify inequality or discrimination.

This creates a necessary distinction between voluntarism in implementation and supremacy in principle. Ambedkar's opt-in model was a method to make reform peaceful, not a reason to tolerate injustice. In other words, the process may be voluntary, but justice cannot be.

Ambedkar's view continues to provide the guiding framework for this debate. His approach suggests that while reform must be carried out through inclusion and dialogue, the end goal remains the same: to ensure that all personal and customary laws conform to the Constitution.

This central question — should personal laws hold greater authority than Fundamental Rights? — is therefore more than just theoretical. It forces India to make a decision between faith-based authority and rights-based citizenship.

---

3. EXAMINING THE CONFLICT BETWEEN FAITH AND CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY

The question — Should community-based personal laws hold greater authority than the Fundamental Rights guaranteed by the Constitution? — must be understood through both historical context and present realities.

3.1 The Colonial Legacy and Ambedkar's Dilemma

During British rule, the Lex Loci Report of 1840 introduced a uniform criminal and contract law but left personal laws untouched to avoid offending religious sentiments. This created a dual legal system: one secular and one religious, which continued after independence.

When India's framers debated the UCC, Dr. Ambedkar strongly supported it, but the trauma of Partition made many leaders cautious. To preserve peace, Article 44 was included in the Directive Principles of State Policy — expressing the "desirability" of a UCC but leaving it non-enforceable.

Ambedkar's intention was to allow social consensus to grow, but he never believed personal laws could stand above Fundamental Rights.

3.2 Inequality Within Personal Laws: A Quick Look

India's personal law systems have historically reflected deep gender and social inequality across all religions. The issue is not with any single community, but with the principle of allowing personal laws to function outside constitutional review. The demand for reform is not "anti-minority"; it is pro-equality and pro-justice.

- Hindu Personal Law: Before reforms (1951–56), practices like polygamy and denial of women's property rights were common. Though reformed through the Hindu Code Bills, some discrimination remains — for example, the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act still names the father as the "natural guardian," making the mother secondary.

- Muslim Personal Law: Muslim law in India remains largely uncodified, based on interpretations of the Quran, Hadith, and customary practices. This has allowed outdated practices such as polygamy for men, unequal inheritance, and previously Triple Talaq (now banned). Women's rights in divorce, custody, and guardianship are still often restricted due to resistance to legislative reform.

- Christian Personal Law: Until 2001, divorce laws under the Indian Divorce Act were discriminatory, giving men more grounds for divorce than women. Reforms have since corrected this, but the delay shows how long gender inequality was justified under the guise of religion.

- Parsi Personal Law: Under Parsi law, women who marry outside the faith lose their religious and property rights, reflecting exclusionary and patriarchal norms that conflict with Articles 14 and 15 of the Constitution.

3.3 Customary and Tribal Laws: Equality vs. Cultural Autonomy

The issue of constitutional supremacy also extends beyond religious personal laws to tribal and customary practices, many of which are recognized under the Fifth and Sixth Schedules of the Constitution, aimed at preserving cultural autonomy. However, when such customs violate Fundamental Rights, courts have intervened to uphold equality and dignity.

- Gender Discrimination in Inheritance or Leadership: In several tribal communities, such as the Khasi, Garo, and Naga, women are denied equal inheritance rights or excluded from leadership positions. These practices directly violate Article 14 (Equality before law) and Article 15 (Prohibition of discrimination based on sex). In Ka Suma Riang v. Ka Mating Rympei (2014), the Meghalaya High Court questioned gender-discriminatory matrilineal inheritance practices under Khasi custom.

- Exclusion of Women from Village Councils: Some traditional governance systems, such as Nagaland's Village Councils, exclude women from decision-making. In 2016, the Nagaland State Women's Commission petitioned for 33% reservation for women in urban local bodies. Tribal groups opposed it, citing "customary law," but the courts held that customary law cannot override constitutional equality.

- Practices Like Witch-Hunting and Forced Marriages: In some tribal regions (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Assam), practices like witch-hunting or forced marriages still persist under traditional customs. These violate Articles 14, 15, 17, and 21 (right to dignity). States such as Jharkhand and Assam have enacted Witch-Hunting (Prohibition) Acts to curb these practices.

3.4 The Judiciary's Role in Protecting Fundamental Rights

The judiciary has made it clear that no personal or customary law can claim supremacy over the Constitution.

- In Shah Bano (1985), the Supreme Court upheld a Muslim woman's right to maintenance under Article 21, emphasizing that personal laws cannot deny dignity.
- In Sarla Mudgal (1995), the Court ruled that conversion to Islam for the purpose of bigamy was unconstitutional under Article 14.
- In Shayara Bano (2017), the Court struck down instant triple talaq, stating that it violated women's equality and dignity.
- In Vineeta Sharma v. Rakesh Sharma (2020), the Supreme Court upheld that daughters have equal coparcenary rights in ancestral property, reinforcing the principle of gender equality in Hindu inheritance law.

Each of these cases reaffirmed one truth: the Constitution, not custom, is the ultimate source of justice.

3.5 The Current Perspective: Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud's View

Chief Justice Chandrachud recently observed that the Constitution expresses the "desirability" of a Uniform Civil Code but emphasized that its implementation must be inclusive and legislative, rather than judicial. He praised the Constitution for binding India's diverse communities and stressed that reform must be guided by dialogue, consensus, and inclusion. He also supported state-level committees studying the UCC, stating that such steps are valid exercises of constitutional power.

Ambedkar's vision and modern realities are balanced in Chandrachud's comments. He recognizes that while uniformity is desirable, the method must be democratic in nature. The Constitution desires equality, but through persuasion, not force.

3.6 The Real Issue: Balance Misunderstood

When Nassa speaks of "balance," it risks being interpreted as compromise, where justice is softened to preserve peace. But true balance is not about giving equal weight to religion and rights; it is about ensuring that religion operates within constitutional limits. Religious freedom must coexist with equality and dignity, not be above them.

Thus, the correct understanding of balance is not "Faith vs. Rights," but "Faith within Rights."

---

4. WHY FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS MUST COME FIRST

4.1 Constitutional Supremacy

India's Constitution already contains the tools for reform:
- Article 13 voids laws that violate fundamental rights.
- Articles 32 and 226 give citizens the right to challenge such laws in court.
- Judicial review ensures that the Constitution always prevails over custom.

Crux: India does not need to wait for a Uniform Civil Code to implement equality. The Constitution itself is the ultimate civil code of rights and freedoms.

4.2 Evidence of Targeted Reforms

India's progress toward equality has already been achieved through targeted reforms:
- Hindu Succession (Amendment) Act, 2005: Gave daughters equal inheritance rights.
- Christian Divorce (Amendment) Act, 2001: Ensured gender equality in divorce.
- Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019: Criminalized instant triple talaq.

These changes prove that reform does not require uniformity; it only requires constitutional commitment.

4.3 Reform with Inclusion

Chief Justice Chandrachud and Ambedkar both remind us that reform must involve dialogue and inclusion. Committees, consultations, and state-level studies are healthy democratic methods. They allow communities to participate in shaping laws that affect them, ensuring that reform does not alienate but unites.

4.4 Beyond Uniformity: The Real Meaning of Secularism

True secularism, as envisioned by Ambedkar and Nehru, is not about erasing religion but about ensuring justice within diversity. Religion may guide personal life, but it cannot authorize injustice. Equality, dignity, and liberty form the uniform minimum for all Indians — the foundation on which personal freedoms can stand.

Reform must therefore be universal in principle, guided by equality and justice, but gradual in method, respecting India's social fabric.

---

5. CONCLUSION: FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS AS THE TRUE UNIFORM CODE

Whether India enacts a Uniform Civil Code or continues reforming personal laws one by one, the ultimate principle must remain the same: fundamental rights are supreme.

Dr. Ambedkar's idea of voluntary reform was never meant to make equality optional; it was meant to achieve it peacefully. The Constitution already provides the moral and legal tools, as outlined in Articles 13, 14, 15, and 21, ensuring that no law or custom can stand if it violates equality, liberty, or human dignity.

Therefore, the debate should not be framed as UCC versus religious pluralism but as a reaffirmation of constitutional supremacy. Uniformity is only a tool; justice is the goal. Every law, personal or state-made, must bend to the higher authority of Fundamental Rights. They are, in essence, the real Uniform Civil Code that already unites all citizens under one Constitution.
