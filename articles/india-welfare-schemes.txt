INDIA'S WELFARE ARCHITECTURE: A CRITICAL REVIEW OF SAGY, NRLM, DAY-NULM, MGNREGA, AND PMJDY
Access, Agency, and the Last-Mile Gap in India's Poverty Reduction Schemes

---

INTRODUCTION

India's approach to poverty reduction has evolved from passive handouts to active, multidimensional frameworks. This response paper examines five major central government schemes — SAGY, NRLM, DAY-NULM, MGNREGA, and PMJDY — analyzing their design, strengths, limitations, and underlying tensions. Together, they reveal a common challenge: access alone, whether to leadership, jobs, training, or bank accounts, does not guarantee empowerment.

---

POLICY 1: SAANSAD ADARSH GRAM YOJANA (SAGY)

Introduction

Launched in October 2014, SAGY is a unique rural development scheme based on Mahatma Gandhi's vision. It puts Members of Parliament (MPs) in the driver's seat. Each MP must identify and adopt a village (Adarsh Gram) to transform it into a "model village."

Target: (2014–2019) Develop three villages — one by 2016 and two more by 2019. (2019–2024): Develop five more villages (1 per year). Total Responsibility: Each MP was expected to develop 8 villages in total by 2024.

Funding: No new money. It works on "Convergence," which means pooling resources from existing schemes (such as MGNREGA) to focus on one village.

Strengths

- Values-Based Development: It doesn't just focus on physical infrastructure such as roads and schools; it also aims to improve hygiene practices, promote gender equality, reduce social discrimination, and foster community ownership. By emphasizing the dignity of labor and social harmony, it aims to transform both the physical and cultural environments of the village.

- Pooling Resources: The scheme requires multiple government departments — Health, Education, Rural Development, Roads, and Drinking Water — to coordinate their plans in the same village. This reduces duplication of efforts and ensures that different services complement each other, creating a more unified development strategy rather than scattered, isolated schemes.

- Technical Help: SAGY partners with engineering and management institutions (like those under AICTE) to bring in technical expertise for designing low-cost housing, watershed structures, sanitation systems, and digital classrooms. Students and faculty often conduct field visits, prepare feasibility reports, and provide innovative solutions that villages normally cannot access.

- Accountability: Since each MP directly adopts a village, their performance becomes visible and politically measurable. This personal stake pushes them to attend Gram Sabha meetings, monitor progress regularly, and ensure faster implementation. Their public reputation becomes linked to how well the village performs under their leadership.

Limitations

- Declining Interest: Data shows high enthusiasm in the beginning (Phase 1), but participation decreased as time passed and dropped drastically by Phase 8. MPs lost interest over time.

- No Dedicated Fund: MPs struggle because there is no specific budget for SAGY. They have to beg and coordinate for funds from other schemes, causing delays.

- Biased Selection and Reinforcement of Hierarchies: Research indicates that MPs often "play safe" by selecting villages that are already somewhat developed, easily accessible, or politically aligned, rather than the remote, backward villages that need the most help.

- Risk of Local Dominance: This selection bias can unintentionally strengthen the dominance of existing local leaders or specific castes. By pouring resources into a village already controlled by a powerful group, the scheme may reinforce their social and political power, further undermining marginalized groups and silencing weaker voices in the community.

- Incomplete Projects: Recent data indicate that achieving 100% completion of development plans is rare.

Critical Analysis

SAGY's shift from "spend more money" to "leadership-driven development" is promising because leadership can coordinate multiple schemes and spark community action. But this creates a sustainability paradox: progress often depends on an individual MP's interest, making gains fragile when political attention fades. Moreover, the scheme values social change, which is difficult to measure, so headline indicators (such as roads and toilets) can mask persistent social problems. To fix this, SAGY needs institutionalized leadership, seed funding, measurable proxies for social values, mixed-method monitoring, and incentives that reward lasting community ownership rather than short-term political wins.

Conclusion

SAGY is a visionary concept that accurately identifies leadership as a crucial missing link in rural development. However, without a dedicated "seed fund" to get things moving and stricter rules to ensure MPs pick backward villages, it risks becoming just a symbolic gesture.

POLICY 2: NATIONAL RURAL LIVELIHOOD MISSION (NRLM)

Introduction

Launched in 2011, NRLM is a massive poverty alleviation program for rural India. It shifted the focus from merely giving handouts to building self-reliance. It organizes the rural poor, especially women, into Self-Help Groups (SHGs) to help them access bank loans and start businesses.

Strengths

- Massive Scale: The number of SHGs exploded from 0.88 lakh (pre-2010) to nearly 36 lakh (2015–2020). It has created one of the world's largest women-led grassroots networks.

- Social Inclusion: Studies show that NRLM has successfully brought Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) households into SHGs in significant numbers. The representation of SC/ST women in SHGs is often equal to or higher than their share in the population. This matters because these groups traditionally face major barriers like social discrimination, limited credit access, and fewer livelihood opportunities, and NRLM provides them a structured support system.

- Income Diversification: Prior to NRLM, many rural poor individuals depended primarily on agriculture or wage labor. After joining SHGs, many households started small shops, goat rearing, tailoring, and food processing, among other activities. This diversification makes income more stable and protects families during agricultural off-seasons or crop failures.

- Financial Access: It connects poor women to formal banks, reducing their dependence on local moneylenders.

Limitations

- Marketing Gap: Women can produce items like pickles, handloom products, and handicrafts, but most SHGs struggle to find reliable markets. Without linkages to larger towns, e-commerce platforms, or retail chains, their products remain local and of low value, limiting income growth.

- Bank Delays: Beneficiaries often face long wait times to get loans approved, which stalls their business plans.

- External Shocks: The NRLM relies heavily on group meetings, and its community mobilization system is vulnerable. During the COVID-19 pandemic, SHG formation dropped drastically because meetings couldn't be held.

Critical Analysis

NRLM suffers from a clear "Distance Penalty." Data shows that families closer to towns or markets benefit more because they can easily access training centers, banks, and marketing hubs. Households in remote villages face higher travel costs and limited exposure to business opportunities.

Additionally, while NRLM performs strongly in increasing income, social empowerment remains uneven. In many groups, women attend meetings but do not actively participate in decisions. Low meeting attendance and limited confidence hinder their ability to influence household and community decisions fully.

Conclusion

The NRLM has mobilized millions of rural women and laid a powerful foundation for their financial and social upliftment. However, to truly transform lives, the mission must now strengthen marketing channels, improve bank responsiveness, and provide additional support to remote areas so that distance and lack of connectivity do not limit progress. With these improvements, NRLM can become a long-term engine of inclusive rural development.

POLICY 3: DAY-NULM (URBAN LIVELIHOOD MISSION)

Introduction

This is the urban counterpart to NRLM, launched in 2013. It targets the urban poor, people experiencing homelessness, and street vendors. The goal is to provide shelter, skill training, and subsidized loans for self-employment.

Strengths

- Multi-Dimensional: It recognizes that urban poverty isn't just about money; it's also about lack of housing (shelters) and identity (social vulnerability). DAY-NULM therefore provides skill training, community groups (SHGs/ALGs), shelters for people experiencing homelessness, and support for street vendors. This multi-layered approach addresses economic, social, and residential needs, rather than focusing solely on one problem.

- Legal Status for Vendors: By issuing identity cards and conducting citywide surveys of vendors, the policy provides street vendors with a formal identity in the urban economy. This reduces police harassment, prevents arbitrary eviction, and allows vendors to access government schemes. It also increases their dignity and bargaining power with local authorities.

- Cheap Loans: Through interest subsidies on loans (approximately 7%), DAY-NULM enables the urban poor to start or expand micro-businesses, such as tailoring, food stalls, repair shops, and beauty services. The lower cost of capital encourages entrepreneurship and helps households build a stable income base.

Limitations

- Implementation Gaps: Performance varies widely across states and cities. Some urban local bodies actively form groups and conduct training sessions, while others fail to complete basic tasks, such as identifying beneficiaries or setting up shelters. This uneven progress reduces the overall impact of the mission.

- Training vs. Jobs: A major mismatch exists between the skills taught and what employers actually need. Many trainees complete the program but remain unemployed because their skills are outdated or no longer in demand. This makes the training component less effective in providing real income opportunities.

- Bureaucracy: Loan processes are often slow and require multiple documents that poor households, especially migrants and homeless people, cannot easily provide. Long verification steps, bank hesitancy, and paperwork discourage many from applying for credit even when they qualify.

Critical Analysis

DAY-NULM attempts to replicate the rural SHG model in urban settings, but cities lack the strong social bonds found in villages. Urban residents often move frequently, work long hours, and have weaker neighbor relationships, making group formation and long-term participation harder.

Additionally, while giving legal ID cards to street vendors is a major step, it has limited value without physical infrastructure. Many cities have still not created proper vending zones with water, lighting, or storage. As a result, vendors continue to face eviction, conflict with authorities, and unstable livelihoods despite having formal recognition.

Conclusion

DAY-NULM is an essential safety net for the urban poor and stands out for giving street vendors long-overdue legal identity. However, to achieve long-term economic improvement, the mission must address the skill–job mismatch and ensure banks lend without excessive paperwork. With better implementation and stronger infrastructure, DAY-NULM can significantly strengthen urban livelihoods.

COMPARING SHGs UNDER NRLM VS. DAY-NULM

Both the Rural (NRLM) and Urban (NULM) missions use Self-Help Groups (SHGs) as a tool, but their roles are very different. In rural areas, the community is the "engine" of the scheme. In urban areas, the community group is a "support" mechanism.

Self-Help Groups in NRLM (Rural):
- The Backbone: The entire scheme depends on SHGs. You cannot have NRLM without them.
- Structure: Highly organized. Small SHGs join together to form Village Organizations, which join to form Cluster Federations.
- Focus: Savings, lending within the group, and social bonding.

Self-Help Groups in DAY-NULM (Urban):
- Support Role: SHGs exist, but the scheme also focuses heavily on individuals (like street vendors) without forcing them into groups.
- Structure: Simpler and less rigid (Area Level Groups).
- Focus: Primarily on getting skill training and bank loans for business.
- Challenge: Social bonding is weaker in cities compared to villages, making these groups harder to sustain.

POLICY 4: MGNREGA (RURAL EMPLOYMENT GUARANTEE)

Introduction

Enacted in 2005, this is the world's most extensive social protection program. It creates a legal "Right to Work." It guarantees 100 days of unskilled wage employment per year to any rural household that asks for it.

Strengths

- Stops Distress Migration: MGNREGA provides work close to home, which prevents families from migrating to faraway cities in search of survival-level income. This helps keep families together and reduces the financial and emotional stress caused by seasonal or long-term migration. In many tribal and drought-prone areas, it has acted as a safety valve during lean agricultural months.

- Gender Equality: MGNREGA guarantees equal wages for men and women, which is rare in rural labor markets where women have traditionally been underpaid. This equal-pay structure has increased women's bargaining power even in private farm work, and it has encouraged more women to join the workforce with dignity and confidence.

- Wage Floor: The program sets a minimum benchmark for rural wages. Private farmers now must match or exceed MGNREGA wages to attract labor, which has contributed to higher agricultural wages across many states. This also reduces exploitation, especially during peak farming seasons.

- Financial Inclusion: Because all wages are paid directly into bank accounts, more rural families have joined the formal financial system. This has reduced their reliance on local moneylenders who charge very high interest rates. Bank accounts also help workers save, receive government benefits, and build financial security.

Limitations

- Supply-Driven Paradox: MGNREGA is supposed to be demand-driven, but in practice, states with weak administrative capacity — often the poorest states — struggle to provide work even when people need it desperately. This results in richer or better-governed states generating more work, creating inequality in scheme benefits.

- Corruption: Although contractors are banned, they often operate indirectly by influencing muster rolls (attendance records) or by using machinery where manual labor is required. This reduces the amount of work available to real workers and diverts funds away from poor households.

- Delayed Payments: Despite a legal requirement for timely payments, wages often arrive weeks or even months late due to paperwork delays, fund shortages, or slow banking processes. When payments are delayed, people lose trust in the scheme and may return to low-paid, exploitative jobs for immediate cash.

Critical Analysis

MGNREGA creates tension with agriculture during peak seasons. Farmers argue that they struggle to find workers for harvesting or sowing because laborers prefer the guaranteed wages under MGNREGA. This forces farmers to pay higher wages or mechanize quickly, which can be particularly challenging for small-scale farmers.

Additionally, while the scheme aims to create durable assets, such as ponds, irrigation channels, and village roads, the quality control is inconsistent. Many assets are poorly planned or supervised, resulting in roads that erode during the monsoon or tanks that do not retain water. This reduces long-term value and limits the productivity benefits the scheme is supposed to generate.

Conclusion

MGNREGA remains one of India's most important social safety nets, protecting rural families from falling into extreme poverty. To improve its effectiveness, the program must become more flexible by finding alternatives for farmers who seek labor during critical agricultural seasons. With better monitoring and stronger local governance, MGNREGA can continue to support rural livelihoods while creating long-lasting community assets.

POLICY 5: PMJDY (FINANCIAL INCLUSION)

Introduction

Launched in 2014, Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) aims to provide every household with a bank account. It serves as the entry point for the "JAM Trinity" (Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar ID, Mobile), enabling the government to send money directly to individuals.

Strengths

- Global Scale: PMJDY has set a Guinness World Record for opening millions of bank accounts in a single day and has now surpassed 48 crore accounts. This level of outreach is unmatched globally, demonstrating the government's ability to mobilize the banking system quickly. It also created a nationwide financial infrastructure almost overnight.

- Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): By linking PMJDY accounts with Aadhaar and mobile numbers, subsidies are now directly transferred to poor households. This has significantly reduced corruption, middlemen, and cash leakages in welfare schemes. For many families, this was the first time they received the full amount of a government benefit without deductions.

- Women's Empowerment: A significant share of PMJDY accounts belong to women, giving them personal control over money that was previously handled mainly by male family members. This boosts women's confidence, improves their bargaining power at home, and encourages independent savings and decision-making.

- Safety Net: PMJDY accounts include accidental insurance coverage and a small overdraft facility (the bank allows PMJDY account holders to borrow a small amount of money, up to Rs. 10,000, even if they have no money in their account, and they can repay it later). For low-income families, this acts as an emergency cushion during accidents or sudden expenses. Even a small overdraft can help households avoid high-interest loans from informal lenders.

Limitations

- Zero-Balance Accounts: Many accounts remain empty because people either lack regular income or do not see the need to save. While account opening is high, account usage remains a challenge, indicating that financial inclusion must extend beyond simply opening accounts.

- Financial Illiteracy: Millions of people received RuPay cards but do not know how to activate them, use ATMs, or remember their PINs. This leads to reliance on banking correspondents or agents, who sometimes charge extra fees or misuse cards, reducing the safety and usefulness of the scheme.

- Private Sector Lag: Public sector banks carried out almost the entire scheme by opening 96% of the accounts. Private banks contributed very little, showing a lack of interest in low-income customers. This places an additional burden on government banks, resulting in uneven access across regions.

Critical Analysis

PMJDY has focused heavily on the supply side — opening accounts, issuing cards, and linking Aadhaar — but has given much less attention to the demand side, which is people's ability to actually use and benefit from these accounts. A bank account alone cannot improve someone's life unless they have income to save or transactions to perform. The real challenge now is not financial access but financial activity. Unless people generate income and gain digital literacy, accounts risk becoming dormant.

Conclusion

PMJDY has successfully established the essential infrastructure for financial inclusion, providing every household with a gateway to formal banking. The next stage is to ensure that people know how to use this system confidently (through financial and digital literacy) and have livelihoods that allow them to save and transact regularly. Only then can PMJDY move from "banking access" to true "financial empowerment."

---

OVERALL CONCLUSION

Taken together, SAGY, NRLM, DAY-NULM, MGNREGA, and PMJDY demonstrate that India has made significant strides toward establishing a comprehensive, multidimensional framework for poverty reduction. Each scheme addresses a distinct aspect: leadership, livelihoods, urban vulnerability, employment security, and financial inclusion. All five have expanded the state's reach into communities that were previously underserved. They have strengthened women's participation, improved access to banking and credit, reduced distress migration, and created institutional platforms such as SHGs and vendor identities that give dignity and voice to marginalized groups.

However, several common challenges run across all policies. First, implementation remains uneven: states with stronger administrative capacity deliver better results, while poorer or remote regions lag behind. Second, many schemes rely on convergence or coordination across departments, which often results in delays, incomplete projects, and inconsistent quality. Third, all five face a "last-mile usage gap": SAGY depends too much on an MP's interest; NRLM and NULM struggle with market linkages and job placements; MGNREGA suffers from delayed payments and asset quality issues; and PMJDY accounts often remain dormant due to low literacy and irregular incomes. These issues show that access alone — whether to leadership, jobs, training, or bank accounts — does not guarantee empowerment.

In conclusion, the five schemes have created an important foundation for reducing multidimensional poverty, but their next stage must focus on deepening impact rather than just expanding coverage. Stronger implementation support for weaker states, improved monitoring, enhanced financial and digital literacy, stronger market linkages, and simplified credit processes are essential. If these systemic gaps are addressed, these policies can function together as a more effective ecosystem for long-term economic and social upliftment.
