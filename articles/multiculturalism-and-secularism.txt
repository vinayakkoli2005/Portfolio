MULTICULTURALISM & SECULARISM
India's Unique Path to Diversity: The Evolving Experiment of Secular Reform

---

When India became independent in 1947, it faced a monumental task to build a single unified policy from one of the most diverse societies on earth — a land of countless languages, faiths, and social groups. Most western democracies like the US or France approached this challenge through a strict separation between church and state. India, however, took a different path. As Rochana Bajpai observes, it was "ahead of its time" — crafting a system that didn't erect a wall between religion and government, but instead built a constitutional framework of engaged secularism.

This model, described by Malcom and Shardool as "principled distance," allowed the state to engage with religion when necessary — sometimes intervening to enact social reform (like untouchability and triple talaq) and at other times protecting a group's autonomy. India's Constitution created one of the most detailed systems in the world for supporting diversity. It includes reservations and separate family laws for different religions.

But beneath this progressive design were major tensions. India has a strong moral reason to help oppressed castes, yet only a weak and politically sensitive reason to protect religious minorities. This imbalance, which Bajpai called "normative imbalance," has influenced India's politics and law for decades and still affects how secularism and multiculturalism work today.

---

RETHINKING SECULARISM AND MULTICULTURALISM

Sune Laegaard provides a useful framework for understanding this tension. He argues that multiculturalism and secularism are not opposing policies, but complementary moral logics. Multiculturalism, often mistaken as "special rights," is better understood as the fair and equal application of universal rights to people in diverse contexts. A Sikh officer wearing a turban with his uniform is not a privilege, but an expression of religious freedom applied equally.

India's problem, therefore, is not the coexistence of secularism and multiculturalism, but the uneven moral justifications underlying them. As Bajpai shows, social justice for caste groups was branded as a strong moral language of equality and national healing. In contrast, rights for religious minorities — such as family laws — were framed as reluctant concessions to preserve stability after partition. This asymmetry leads to the caste system being labelled as progressive, while religious accommodation remained politically vulnerable, easily attacked as "appeasement."

---

FROM CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY TO COURTS

This imbalance was embedded during the founding debates of the Assembly (1946–49). Scarred by partition and fear of repeating colonial "divide and rule," India's leaders feared policies that visibly separated citizens by religion. As a result, the Constitution enshrined two distinct rationales for intervention — one approach was moral and unifying (caste inequality), and the other was cautious and temporary (religious differences). Over time, this difference became part of the legal and judicial system.

As Katrak and Kulkarni explain, the judiciary became the chief arbiter of "principled distance." To decide which religious practices deserved protection, courts developed the essential practices doctrine — empowering judges to determine what is truly "religious" and what may be regulated. This gave secular courts the power to decide whether a practice was "essential" to faith or a "superstition" the state could reform. Predictably, the doctrine was applied unevenly. State intervention in Hindu customs, justified by social reforms, was seen as legitimate, while reluctance to intervene in minority laws like Muslim family law was seen as protecting minorities. The problem was not intervention itself, but the unequal reasoning behind it — a symptom of India's foundational normative imbalance.

Sohaira Siddiqui's research on the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act (2019), which criminalized instant triple talaq, shows how India's secular democracy is evolving to uphold gender equality within religion. Celebrated as a landmark victory for Muslim women, by ending instant triple talaq the state reaffirmed its duty to ensure all women, regardless of faith, enjoy dignity.

Her analysis also reveals the double bind of India's secularism. When the state reforms Hindu customs, it is praised as progressive, but when intervening in Muslim law, it faces charges of majoritarianism. The 2019 Act resolved this paradox, yet still advances the constitutional goal of gender justice. Siddiqui argues that the reform aligns personal law with the principles of justice, equality, and dignity. Even if triple talaq was rare, it represented a deep gender inequality. This Act's importance is both symbolic and practical — it makes clear that no religious custom can override constitutional rights.

---

THE PANDEMIC TEST AND POLITICS OF SELECTIVITY

The weakness of India's moral balance was again revealed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Katrak and Kulkarni expose how the government briefly adopted a strict "separation" model, banning all religious gatherings. However, the rules were not equally enforced. The Muslim Tablighi Jamaat event in Delhi was labelled as "super-spreaders" and blamed for spreading the virus, while Hindu gatherings like Ram Yatra were later allowed after political pressure with some conditions. This uneven enforcement, presented as health policy but driven by politics, showed how fragile the idea of equal respect for all religions can be when protection for minorities is weak.

Yet India's system still demonstrated its strength. The Bombay High Court later dismissed the case against Tablighi Jamaat attendees, criticizing the "smell of malice" behind the charges. This shows that even when politics fails, India's judiciary acts as a self-correcting force, helping restore the fairness promised by the Constitution.

---

CONCLUSION

Together, the works of Bajpai, Laegaard, Katrak, Kulkarni, and Siddiqui show that India's secularism is not a fixed model, but an evolving experiment. Its strength lies in its ability to adapt — balancing moral reform with respect for cultural diversity. However, deep tensions remain. The divide between social justice for caste and religious autonomy continues to shape key debates, from temple reform to triple talaq.

To progress, India must build a unified foundation for group rights based on constitutional morality rather than fear or political convenience. True reforms, as Siddiqui notes, should empower communities through dialogue and gender equality, not punishment. Likewise, Laegaard reminds us that recognizing difference is part of achieving real substantive equality.

India's secularism is distinct — neither western nor rigid, but a living moral project shaped by its history and democracy. Its next step is to turn "principled distance" into "principled fairness," ensuring that engagement with religion upholds liberty, equality, and fraternity for all.
